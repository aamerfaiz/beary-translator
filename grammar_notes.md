# Beary (Byari) — Phonology & Grammar Notes

Everything below is taken from the front matter of the **Beary–Kannada–English
Lexicon** (*Nigantu*), Karnataka Beary Sahitya Academy, 2017 — the phonology
note by Prof. B.A. Viveka Rai and the Editorial by B.M. Ichlangod,
B.A. Shamshuddin Madikeri and Abdul Rahman Kuthethoor. Page references are to
the Roman-numeral front matter (`docs/PronunciationandStuff.PDF`).

This file supersedes an earlier version of these notes that was assembled from
web sources and inference. Where the two disagreed, the Academy's text wins.

## 1. What Beary is

- A **Dravidian language** spoken by the Beary community of coastal Karnataka
  (Dakshina Kannada, Udupi) and Kasaragod in Kerala, by roughly 1.5 million
  people. The Academy's editors describe it as an independent Dravidian
  language and "one of the sister languages of Kannada", alongside Tulu,
  Konkani and Kodava.
- It has borrowed from **Arabic** (religious and household vocabulary),
  **Tamil, Tulu, Malayalam, Kannada** and modern **English**. The editors are
  explicit that borrowed words are treated as Beary words, not foreign ones —
  *Computer*, *College* (English), *Bojja*, *Nema* (Tulu), *Upakara*,
  *Prayoga* (Kannada) all appear as headwords.
- Historically a **spoken** language with no script of its own. Bearys write it
  in Kannada script; the Lexicon adds a Roman transliteration column so that
  speakers outside Karnataka, and non-Kannada readers, can use it. **This
  project uses only that Roman transliteration.**

> Note: an earlier version of these notes called Beary "a Malayalam dialect
> with Tulu phonology". The Academy does not describe it that way. Many Beary
> words are pronounced like Malayalam words because of the community's
> religious ties to Kerala, but that is contact, not descent.

## 2. Sound inventory (pp. xxxix–xlii)

**Vowels — 10, plus 2 special.**

|        | Front | Central | Back |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | i ī | ụ | u ū |
| Mid  | e ē |     | o ō |
| Low  | ɛ   | a ā |      |

Rounded: `u ū ụ o ō`. Unrounded: `i ī e ē a ā`.
The two special vowels are **ụ** (central, high) and **ɛ** (low front).

**Consonants — 23, including 6 nasals.**

|            | Voiceless | Voiced | Nasal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Velar      | k  | g  | ṅ |
| Palatal    | c  | j  | ñ |
| Retroflex  | ṭ  | ḍ  | ṇ |
| Dental     | t  | d  | n, n' |
| Labial     | p  | b  | m |

Unclassified: `y r l v ś s h`.
Created for Arabic loanwords: `kh`, `jh`, `ph` — pronounced unlike `k`, `j`, `p`.

**There are no aspirated sounds in native Beary words.**

`n'` is a distinct alveolar nasal, separate from `n`, and it is contrastive:
*n'ōkụ* (see), *n'āvu* (tongue).

## 3. Pronunciation key (p. xli–xlii)

| | nearest English | | nearest English |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | *u* of "cut"    | ṭ  | *t* of "neat" (retroflex) |
| ā | *a* of "calm"   | ḍ  | *d* of "day" (retroflex) |
| i | *i* of "sit"    | ṇ  | no English equivalent (retroflex) |
| ī | *ee* of "keep"  | n' | *n* of "synthetic", "anthem" |
| u | *u* of "put"    | t  | *th* of "thief" |
| ū | *oo* of "shoot" | d  | *th* of "they" |
| ɛ | *e* of "men"    | ś  | *sh* of "shame" |
| e | *e* of "bet"    | kh | no English equivalent; Arabic *kh* in "Khalifa" |
| ē | *a* of "mate"   | jh | *z* of "zero" |
| o | *o* of "obey"   | ph | *f* of "father" |
| ō | *o* of "note"   | ṅ  | *ng* of "sing" |
| c | *ch* of "chicken" | ñ | *n* of "bench" |

`ṭ ḍ ṇ` are retroflex — the tongue tip curls back. English has no retroflex
consonants, so those equivalents are approximate.

**Anusvara.** Written `o` in Kannada script; in transliteration it becomes the
nasal matching the following consonant: `ṅ` before k/g (and kh), `ñ` before c/j
(and jh), `ṇ` before ṭ/ḍ, `n`/`n'` before t/d, and `m` before p, b, y, r, l, v,
ś, s, h (and ph).

## 4. Word formation (p. xlvii)

**Feminine — `-ti`:**
sorakāra (male worker) → sorakārti · pirānda (mad man) → pirānti ·
yajamāna (male owner) → yajamānti

**Plural — `-ṅ`, `-mār`, `-rụ`:**
dāda (elder sister) → dādaḍaṅa · caṅṅāyi (friend) → caṅṅāyimārụ ·
bālakkāra (young person) → bālakkārụ · pōṇḍa (he goes) → pōṇḍārụ (they go)

**Causative — `-bāṭụ` / `-pāṭụ`** (interchangeable):
ākụ (to do) → ākụbāṭụ / ākụpāṭụ (to make someone do) ·
ōḍu (to run) → ōḍụpāṭụ / ōḍụbāṭụ

**Interchangeable endings `-ro`/`-ḍo` and `-re`/`-ḍe`:**
cellụḍo = cellụro (saying) · ākụḍo = ākụro (doing) ·
pandurɛ = panduḍɛ (flicker) · piḍikurɛ = piḍikuḍɛ (I hold / I catch)

## 5. Sentence structure

Beary follows the general Dravidian pattern: **Subject–Object–Verb**,
postpositions rather than prepositions, and no definite or indefinite articles.

The Academy notes that no grammar of Beary has yet been written, and that the
Lexicon marks only **nouns and verbs**; pronouns, adjectives, adverbs and
interjections are deliberately left untagged. So the dictionary data in this
repo has a part of speech for roughly 86% of entries and blank for the rest —
that blank means "not marked in the source", not "unknown word".

## 6. Things the editors flag as limitations

- Beary has **considerable regional variation** (Mangalore, Udupi/Kundapura to
  the north, Kumble/Manjeshwara/Kasargod/Vittla/Puttur/Sullia to the south).
  The Lexicon deliberately includes variants rather than picking one, which is
  why many entries carry `see_also` forms with the same gloss.
- Many words carry **more than one meaning**, more so than in neighbouring
  languages, and the editors kept all of them.
- Some vocabulary has been **lost** with the decline of traditional crafts and
  household objects. The editors note there is no Beary word for "heart".
- The Lexicon documents **about 20,000 words** and the editors describe it as a
  first pass, expecting corrections in later editions.

## 7. Paradigms recovered from the Lexicon

The Academy states that no grammar of Beary has been written, and the Lexicon
is a word list, not a grammar. But a word list of 17,234 entries glossed in
English contains a great deal of grammar implicitly: an entry glossed "To him"
is a dative pronoun whether or not anyone labelled it one.

Everything below was mined from `data/dictionary.json` by
`tools/build_grammar_pack.py` and is an attested headword of the Lexicon. Each
form in `data/grammar_pack.json` carries the gloss and page it came from, so
any line here can be checked against the printed dictionary.

One filter does most of the work. The Academy tags **only nouns and verbs**,
leaving "pronoun, adjective, adverb, interjections" untagged — so an untagged
entry is the more likely function word, and a noun- or verb-tagged entry that
matched a one-word gloss is usually English ambiguity. That is what separates
the possessive "mine" from `kani`, `gani`, a mine you dig, and the postposition
"like" from `meccụ`, the verb.

| meaning | Beary |

| I / me | ñānụ, n'ān'ụ |
| you (singular) | n'iṅa, n'iṅalụ, n'ī, n'īnụ |
| he | avonu, avnu, ānu, ōnu |
| she | avolu, īma, ōlu, vōlu |
| we | ñaṅa, n'aṅa, ñammo |
| they | aṅa, avorụ, ōrụ, ayālụ |
| this | ī, idụ |
| that | adụ, ā |
| oneself | tānē |
| to me (dative) | n'akkụ |
| to you (dative) | n'iṅkụ, n'ikkụ |
| to him (dative) | ōnugu |
| to them (dative) | aṅogu, aṅkụ(ku) |
| us (accusative) | n'aṅale |
| your / yours (genitive) | niṇḍo, n'iṅalo |
| his (genitive) | avu(vo)ṇḍo, ōnḍo |
| her (genitive) | ōlo, ōlɛ |

Case is marked by a suffix, and the pattern is visible across the paradigm:

- **dative** `-kụ` / `-gu` — n'akkụ (to me), n'iṅkụ (to you), ōnugu (to him), aṅogu (to them)
- **genitive** `-ḍo` / `-o` — n'aṇḍo (my), niṇḍo (your), ōnḍo (his), ōlo (her)
- **accusative** `-e` / `-le` — n'aṅale (us)

Note how prominent the distinct alveolar nasal `n'` is in this paradigm. It is
not interchangeable with `n`.

Interrogatives are built on a common frame: most begin with **e-**
(eññɛ, eṇḍụ, endi, eṅanɛ, ettarɛ, endo), and the who/whose group begins with
**ā-** (ārụ, āre, āro). The Lexicon prints them with a following "?".

## 8. What the Lexicon does not tell us

**Verb inflection.** The Lexicon lists verbs in a citation form only, and
tense/person marking is not recoverable from it. Only a couple of entries hint
at it at all (uṇḍɛ "I have eaten", beccɛ "I have kept it"). Beary verb citation
forms end overwhelmingly in **-kụ** (417), **-ṭụ** (343) and **-ḍụ** (134).

This was the single biggest gap, and it could not be filled from the printed
sources. A speaker has since supplied the paradigm — see §10. What is there
covers two verbs, so the gap is narrowed, not closed.

## 9. On using Tulu as a model

Beary and Tulu share sentence structure — verb last, relators after the noun,
case as a suffix — and Tulu's grammar is far better documented. That structure
is worth borrowing.

Tulu **words** are not. Tested against this Lexicon, only 23% of the Tulu forms
in a published Tulu grammar scaffold appear in Beary at all, and most of those
that do mean something else entirely:

| Tulu | means | but Beary | means |
|---|---|---|---|
| piravu | behind | pirāvu | pigeon |
| nama | we (inclusive) | nāma | mark worn on the forehead |
| enna | my | enna | counting |
| idi | throughout | iḍi | thunder |
| muta | up to, till | mūta | elder |
| aye | he (remote) | āyɛ̄ | did you become? |

Tulu's entire case-marked pronoun paradigm (yAn, enk, enna, ninan, nikk, imbe,
akulu, eer) is absent from Beary, which has its own, given above. Tulu's verb
conjugation classes key on verbs ending in *-pu* and *-N*, which does not
describe Beary verbs either.

So: take the sentence pattern from Tulu, take every actual word from the
Lexicon, and never the other way round.

## 10. Grammar from a speaker

The Lexicon is a word list. It has no conjugation, and the Academy says
outright that no Beary grammar has been written. Everything in this section
came from a native speaker of the **Mangalore city** variety (August 2026).
Every stem and pronoun in it was then checked against the Lexicon and matches;
the inflected forms are the speaker's, because the dictionary has none.

Treat this as reliable in shape and thin in coverage: the verb paradigm rests
on two verbs from one speaker.

### Case

| case | suffix | examples |
|---|---|---|
| dative (to, for) | `-kụ`, `-gụ` | n'akkụ (to me) · n'ikkụ (to you) · ōnugu (to him) · ceṅṅāyigụ (to a friend) |
| genitive ('s) | `-ḍo`, `-ro` | n'aṇḍo (my) · ōnḍo (his) · abbaro (father's) · avtuḍo ~ avturo (of the house) |
| locative (in, at) | `-lụ` | adụ "that" → adụlụ "in that" · avtu "house" → avtulu "at home" |

The genitive attaches to ordinary nouns, not just pronouns. The Lexicon
corroborates all three: `n'akkụ` p421, `n'ikkụ` p431, `ōnugu` p126, `n'aṇḍo`
p421, `adụlụ` p16, `avtulu` p40.

### Verbs: stem + tense marker + person ending

**1st and 2nd person are always identical.** Person is carried by the pronoun,
not the verb. Only the 3rd person is marked, with `-a`.

| | past `-y-` | future `-nd-` | immediate `-nd-` + `-o` |
|---|---|---|---|
| naa (I) | poye | ponday | pondo |
| nee (you) | poye | ponday | pondo |
| awnu (he) | poy**a** | pond**a** | — |
| nanga (we) | — | poi | — |

Also: `po` bare/imperative; `poyont ullɛ` progressive with an auxiliary, with
`ullar` as the respectful form used for elders.

The same shape holds for another verb: `koḍu` "give" → `kodte` "I gave"
(`koḍ` + `t` + `e`). The tense consonant varies by stem — `-y-` for pō, `-t-`
for koḍ — which is what Tulu does too.

### Negation

Suffix the negative to the stem; there is no separate word.
`piḍi` "knowledge" + `illɛ` "no" → **`piḍillɛ`** "does not know" — and
`piḍillɛ` is itself a Lexicon headword (p485), with `piḍi` p484 sense 3 glossed
"Knowledge". The negative does not change for person.

### Dative subjects

Experiencer verbs take a **dative** subject, not a nominative one:

- `nak piḍillɛ` — "I don't know", literally *to me, no knowledge*
- `nik piḍillɛ?` — "don't you know?"

The verb is identical in both; only the pronoun changes. This is the clearest
demonstration that Beary does not mark person on the verb.

### Questions

**Yes/no** — add `-a` to the final word. It fuses with the last vowel instead of
standing alone, and after a final `-a` a linking `-n-` appears:

- `book avtulu undu` → `book avtulu **unda**?` "is the book in the house?"
- `awnu poya` → `awnu **poyana**?` "did he go?"

This is the same clitic as Tamil and Kannada `-ā`.

**Wh-questions** take no `-a`: question word first, everything else in normal
order, verb still last — `yawde pondo?` "where are you going?"

## 11. The ḍo / ro split is regional

The Academy calls `-ro` and `-ḍo` "used with the same meaning" and never says
who uses which. They are **regional**:

- **`-ḍo`** — north, Udupi and beyond
- **`-ro`** — Mangalore city and Ullal

One phonological rule overrides the choice: **after a nasal it is always
`-ḍo`**, in both varieties. `ro` is simply impossible there, which is why
`n'aṇḍo` "my" is never `n'aṇro`.

The Lexicon confirms this without exception:

| | count |
|---|---|
| words ending `-ro` preceded by a nasal | **0** of 159 |
| bracketed `ḍo(ro)` alternations preceded by a nasal | **0** of 980 |
| bracketed alternations preceded by a vowel | **980** of 980 |

So the alternation applies exactly after a vowel, and the editors' bracket
notation only ever appears where `ro` is possible. That makes the two varieties
mechanically convertible: `abbaḍo` ↔ `abbaro`, `avtuḍo` ↔ `avturo`, while
`n'aṇḍo` stays put. It also matches the Academy's own worked example on
p. xlvii, `pandurɛ` = `panduḍɛ`.

1,244 dictionary entries are affected — 980 the Lexicon brackets, plus 264
recorded in one form only, which can now be converted rather than left as-is.
